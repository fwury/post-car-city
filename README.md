# Post-car city generator

A seeded map of a city built to the Post-Car City plan. Stages 1 to 4 are in place, and stage 5 is under way.

- **The site.** Set the city's population in the panel (150 000 to 10 million; one district to every 150 000 people or so, each housing about that many, the larger ones built less densely). The city is the districts whose cores lie nearest the centre, by a distance stretched a little by direction so the city is not round; round it is country, and the city's edge is a greenway like any other. Each seed picks a kind of site, in about the shares the world's major cities have them: on a river (three in ten: Paris, London, Cairo), where two rivers meet (Pittsburgh, Belgrade, Khartoum), where a river flows into the ocean or a great lake (New York, Shanghai, Buenos Aires), on the shore of one (Toronto, San Francisco, Rio, Athens), or on flat land with no river (Mexico City). A shore may have a peninsula the city stands on or a headland beside it, and an island cut off by a channel; an ocean shore has beaches, sand from the water's edge with the promenade behind it; and about one city in five has a mountain range on one side, its contours shaded, where the city stops. Districts do not grow across a river or channel, so a boundary forms along each; it is laid on the water and widened to hold it, with a green bank and a path along each side, and the streets and rail lines that cross it are bridges. Districts stop at the sea, with a waterfront park and a promenade along the shore.

- **Districts.** District cores come first, spread like a loose Poisson field with varied spacing. Districts grow outward from each core over 400 m land parcels, and where districts meet the boundary becomes a primary greenway. Boundaries carry detail at several scales: broad sweeps and meanders from layered warps, then straight runs of 110 to 340 m with rounded bends, and small swells and pinches in width. Greenways carry no streams.
- **Boulevards.** Tram boulevards link the cores of neighbouring districts. Each link crosses the shared greenway near its middle at right angles; inside a district the links join through the station or end at T junctions (roundabouts are kept for roads outside the city). Boulevards and main streets that meet on a greenway carry on under it, drawn as tunnels.
- **District parks** of 40 to 70 ha sit where three districts meet, spread out evenly. Each is bounded by streets: a few long straight sides meeting at corners, held back from boulevards and cores. Inside, as in High Park or Central Park, a broad loop drive runs round the park well in from its edge; entrance paths curve in from gates at its corners and along its sides to join the loop at an easy angle; winding walks cross the middle between fields, courts, a pond with a path round its shore, and the rest, each with a short spur from the nearest path.
- **Neighbourhoods.** Each is planned for about 15 000 people, enough to support its own shops, schools and services. Density varies, so areas do too: the mid-rise core around the station is densest, boulevards a little denser, and each neighbourhood is built in one form: the less dense (under 18 500 people per km²) are rowhouses throughout, with mixed-use blocks along their main streets, the rest perimeter blocks. Houses of three storeys or more are split into flats, as plexes are. About one district in three has a campus neighbourhood (university or hospital) that houses fewer people for its size. A district gets as many neighbourhoods as its people fill. Every neighbourhood gets a main street through its centre first, and is then regrown outward from that street, so the main street nearest a point decides which neighbourhood it is in and boundaries fall between main streets. Hovering shows a neighbourhood's housing, population and density. Close in, once blocks are in, every block belongs to one neighbourhood, so boundaries run down the middle of streets (smoothed so they do not step round single blocks), and each neighbourhood is planned for the people its own blocks hold.
- **Main streets** (31 m, tram) are old roads between places, forming an organic grid. Every neighbourhood gets one route through its centre (at least one main street, and rarely more than three), laid from the core outwards: its inner half heads for the core and ends in a T on the first street that carries on past it, and its outer half runs on to the next neighbourhood further out, or to a gateway on the greenway (one per shared greenway, two on long ones), where it carries on into the next district. A neighbourhood still without one gets a cross street through its centre; in a wedge of land between two greenways it may end at the gate of the district park they meet at. A smooth direction field follows the boulevards and greenway edges, leans towards each core and drifts slowly elsewhere, so streets bend gently and meet mostly at right angles. No main street dead-ends, curls round or runs alongside another, and junctions keep clear of each other. With about 15 000 people to a neighbourhood that comes to roughly 1.8 to 2 km of main street and boulevard per km² (a main street every kilometre or so), with nearly all land within 600 m of one.
- **Local streets and blocks** (stage 4, from 0.25 px/m in). Streets come first, as centrelines, and each takes its own width out of the land either side, so a block is what is left between the building lines and a street is exactly the space between buildings. Main streets, boulevards and the streets along every greenway and park cut a district into superblocks; each is split again and again by a local street running across its long side, curving to meet the streets at its ends square on and carrying on any street already meeting it from outside, until the pieces are the size their housing calls for: perimeter blocks of about 100 m, rowhouse land cut into strips two houses deep (about 74 m street to street, a lane down the middle) with cross streets every 100 to 175 m, larger podium blocks round the station, campus blocks. Every local street ends on another street; junctions are crossings and T junctions at about right angles, with no short jogs. Each neighbourhood's square (with a market) fronts its main street by its centre. The district centre is a pedestrian zone: every local street within 300 m of its square is given over to people on foot and on bikes, main streets and boulevards still crossing it. A square may stand on its own with a pedestrian street off it, or take in the local street on one side, or on two adjacent sides, so it runs right up to the buildings of the blocks across; a street it takes in goes on beyond it as a pedestrian street. Each neighbourhood's park and secondary school sit side by side, set back from the main streets. Local streets show their pavements close in.
- **Plots** (from 1 px/m in). A perimeter block is several buildings round a courtyard, with gaps of 4.5 to 6.5 m between them, at least one on every street, open through to the courtyard. In the courtyard a path runs round along the backs of the buildings, and paths cross the green from the gaps to a small square in the middle. Plots are cut square to the street (and in a block with no courtyard, square to its long sides), narrower for mixed use on a main street, with corner plots wrapping round their corners. A rowhouse block splits down its spine into two terraces of houses 5 to 8 m wide, in Toronto's proportions: a 3 to 5 m front garden, a house 14 to 17 m deep, a short backyard opening onto a 5 m lane down the spine, running street to street. Corner houses face the long street. Rowhouse blocks are smaller than perimeter blocks (about 200 by 86 m to the street centrelines), so rowhouse areas have more streets; a block too far from rectangular for terraces is split again, and a small awkward leftover is a green. Podium blocks in the core are cut into a few big plots.
- **Buildings** (stage 5, from 1 px/m in). Each building stands on a run of plots (between two gaps round a courtyard, or a whole terrace) and has one height, style and roof, ridges running along the street: a footprint, a height from its storeys, an architectural style, and a roof. On main streets buildings stand on the building line; on quieter streets a few step back a metre or two and their backs vary in depth; rowhouses have front gardens; in the core a building takes the whole block, with no courtyard (or on a longer block several stand side by side, a 6 m walkway between each two): a podium of four to six storeys, its ground floor let to many shops and every floor above to several firms, and over most of them near the station a tower of flats, set back on the podium. Styles are traditional European for the most part (Georgian, Victorian, neoclassical, Renaissance and Baroque revival, Beaux-Arts, Art Nouveau, Art Deco): each district has a family of them, and each neighbourhood was built mostly in one (about four blocks in five; another from the family for most of the rest, and a little modern infill), a block in one style with neighbouring shades of one palette, so streets read as of a piece; its churches, schools and civic buildings keep styles of their own. Schools, hospitals, recreation centres and towers are modern; churches have spires, mosques domes and minarets, and each district has a cathedral, cross-shaped in its close beside a square near the core. Under Buildings in the panel, heights show as shadows (2D) or as raised buildings seen from the south (3D), with roofs and walls in their materials or plain. Places are marked as map apps mark them, a coloured badge with a glyph by kind (schools, health, police, fire, civic, culture, worship, sport, shops, food, lodging) and its kind written beside it close in: the district's own (hospital, stadium, museum, police and fire stations, cathedral...) from middling zoom, each neighbourhood's own (schools, churches, libraries, post office...) closer, everyday shops and cafés only close in. Tap a marker for a card with what it is and the building it is in.
- **Streets to scale** (from 1.1 px/m in). Every street is drawn as its cross-section from the street types: pavements, tree strips with street trees, cycle tracks, kerbs, traffic lanes, tram tracks with their rails, planted medians; junctions are paved across. Tram stops are drawn with the junctions (below); farther out, they show as small markers. In the core neighbourhood, main streets widen to core main streets (37 m, a frontage zone under the podiums) and local streets are core residential streets (26.8 m, with cycle lanes).
- **Greenways and parks** (stage 5). A primary greenway is left natural but for a stream and a trail. The stream runs down the middle, drifting a little off it and back as a small river does, and takes heavy rain off the streets; where greenways meet, the two running most nearly straight on carry the stream through and any other curves round to join it a little downstream, as a tributary does, and their trails meet there too; the trail, wide enough for people walking and cycling both ways, follows it loosely on one side; Local streets that meet a greenway send a path down to the trail every 140 m or so, and each pavement of a street dipping under a greenway has a path down to it. Parkettes have a path and, in the bigger ones, a playground. A neighbourhood park is a mesh of paths, from entrances at its corners and along its sides to junctions through the park, with the neighbourhood's community centre built into its edge on a street. A school's grounds take its track and field (or a field) first, and the building stands in the room left, near the street. District parks are left natural, after Hampstead Heath, Richmond Park or the Bois de Vincennes: open meadow, stands of woodland over about a third of them and one to three ponds (never in a river running through), with paths from gates round the edge winding between them; nothing built, no pitches or courts. Some neighbourhood boundaries (not most: usually a boundary is just a street) are secondary greenways: linear parks 28 to 40 m wide running from one main street to the next, or to a primary greenway, along the boundary, with a trail down the middle and trees along both sides; streets run along their sides and local streets meet them rather than crossing, with a path across where they do. More than half the districts have a stadium (also a concert venue) on a block of its own near the core: an oval, a rounded rectangle or a horseshoe of stands round a pitch.
- **Rail and metro** (stage 5). Both are planned for the whole city at once and judged as networks, not laid line by line. Regional rail in a large city (about 20 districts and up) has a loop line through the central station and the districts round the heart of the city, as the Yamanote Line runs through Tokyo: radial lines peel off it at its stations and run out into the country, dividing at most twice further out and only ever two ways, and their trains come in along the loop through the centre and out along a branch on the far side, so any train can reach any part of the network on its own track. A smaller city has one trunk through the central station instead, dividing into branches either side. Lines meet side by side, along the same alignment, rather than crossing at an angle, and keep well apart between stations; nearly every district has a station in it or next door. Track is laid for 140 km/h: straight along the platforms, curves no tighter than 900 m. Through the centre the lines run in tunnel; out from it they run on viaducts, over a path for walking and cycling with stalls in the arches and streets crossing beneath every 160 m or so, but every station is underground, the track ramping down to it. There is no station building: the ground floors of buildings along both sides of the platforms are given over to entrances (fare gates, stairs and lifts down to the concourse), six to a station and ten at the central station, spread from one end of the platforms to the other, with one more in the district's plaza when it is close; each is marked on the map. The central station has a platform track for about every line, fanned out from the approach tracks with ladders of crossovers at each end. The metro runs in tunnels under the boulevards, from district centre to district centre: long lines across the city through the centre first, then shorter ones between them, each smooth (no curve under 300 m), with two ends, never doubling back. Lines meet at interchanges at district centres, two to a station (three at the city centre), and a line ending one link short of another line or a rail station is carried on to it. Stations are about 800 m apart, about one for every 30 000 people, and become neighbourhood centres; subdistrict centres (libraries and the like) and campuses go where the metro is. The network is judged on how many pairs of districts are a single change apart at most, counting the rail (80 to 95 per cent), how many districts and rail stations it serves, and how close it comes to that station count. Each line has its colour and its letter (rail) or number (metro), shown beside every station close in. Both show under Transit in the panel, and can be hidden there.
- **Intersections** (stage 5), laid out as the street and intersection spec has them, by the streets that meet (boulevard, main street, core street, local street, laneway, in that order: the higher keeps its section through the junction). Tram streets meeting are signalised, with a Dutch protected layout: a corner island at each corner, each street's cycle lane swung out round it to a cycle crossing set back 5 m from the carriageway being turned out of, a 4 m zebra beside it (farther out) crossing the whole street with refuges through medians and buffers and tactile paving at its ends, stop lines 1 m outside, signals on the islands and refuges; tram tracks run straight through, crossing in diamonds. No left turns are drawn, and there are no turn lanes. A side street meeting a tram street is signalised the same way where a tram stop is; elsewhere the tram street's pavement and cycle lane run on unbroken across its mouth (a continuous sidewalk), cars ramping up through the buffer and giving way at the cycle lane, with a zebra over the tram street beside it, so there is a crossing at least every 115 m. Core streets meet on raised tables; local streets on flush plateaus paved in a pattern of their own, some with a line of bollards across to keep cars from cutting through; a pedestrian street ends at a tram street with its bike lanes crossing the pavement to the cycle lanes behind a line of bollards; a laneway opens only on a local street, with bollards just inside it. Where the path under a viaduct or a linear park crosses a tram street, the crossing is signalised, in its favour; across a local street it is marked on the flush surface. Junctions have at most four arms, meeting at 60 to 120 degrees. Tram stops come every 300 to 400 m, on the far side of a signalised junction (or a signalised crossing of their own where there is none): an island platform over a main street's median, or on a boulevard a side platform for each direction in place of the tree buffer, each on its own far side; the tracks never shift. Each platform has a tactile edge, a shelter and a ramp at the junction end.
- **Names** (stage 5). Districts, neighbourhoods and streets get English names, put together from word lists the way English names are made: old place names (a root and an ending, as Ashford or Kingsley; or Market Heath, Old Thornwick), family names, trees and landmarks. Boulevards are Avenues or Boulevards; main streets are Streets, and keep their name across a greenway; local streets are mostly Streets, with the odd Way or Road, Lanes, Mews and Places when short, Crescents when curved; pedestrian streets are Walks or Rows. No two streets in a district share a name. Labels follow their streets and keep the right way up; district names show far out, neighbourhood names in between, street names close in.
- **What is where**, following the urban design guide. Plots on main streets, boulevards, pedestrian streets and squares are mixed use: a shop floor, offices or studios above, homes over that; off them, quieter shop floors on corners and some other plots; in rowhouse areas only corner shops, and a parkette every 250 m or so. Every neighbourhood has its square and market, park, secondary school, two elementary schools, community centre, inn, places of worship (in about Toronto's mix), post office, food bank, health, arts, entertainment, food, shops and services along its main street; about one in three is a subdistrict centre (library, fire station, recreation centre, performing arts, cinema); the core has the district's own (hotels, office towers, police, library, museum, hospitals nearby, now and then a department store or courthouse). Every home is within 400 m of a grocery, convenience store, café, bank, gym, daycare, clinic and pharmacy. Storeys are set so each neighbourhood houses about the people it was planned for.

Far-zoom tiles render in Web Workers.

## Run

ES modules need a server, so `file://` won't work:

```
python3 -m http.server 8123
```

Then open http://localhost:8123. Views are shareable: `?seed=1&pop=1500000&x=1400&y=1400&zoom=0.05`.

## Publish

A GitHub Pages workflow (`.github/workflows/pages.yml`) runs the tests and publishes the site on every push to `main`. Once, in the repository settings under Pages, set Source to GitHub Actions. The site then lives at `https://<user>.github.io/post-car-city/`.

## Test

```
node --test --test-concurrency=1
```

## Conventions

- Units are metres. World x grows east, y grows south (north is up).
- No `Math.random()` in generation. Use `hash(seed, layer, ...ints)` or `rngFor(...)` from `src/core`.
- Generation lives in `src/core` and `src/world`; drawing in `src/render`; interface in `src/ui`.

## Colours

The palette (`src/render/styles.js`) follows Apple Maps: warm off-white land, soft greens for parks (deeper for the large ones), clear light blue water, white main streets with light casings and pale yellow boulevards, light stone buildings whose materials are muted toward it, and place markers in a colour by kind.
