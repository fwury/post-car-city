# Post-car city generator

A seeded map of a city built to the Post-Car City plan. Stages 1 to 4 are in place, and stage 5 is under way.

- **The site.** Set the city's population in the panel (150 000 to 10 million; one district to every 150 000 people or so). The city is the districts whose cores lie nearest the centre, by a distance stretched a little by direction so the city is not round; round it is country, and the city's edge is a greenway like any other. Each seed picks a kind of site, in about the shares the world's major cities have them: on a river (about a third, as London), where two rivers meet (Chongqing), on a lake shore (Toronto), on an ocean coast (Rio), where a river meets the sea (Shanghai), on a peninsula (San Francisco), or inland with no river (Mexico City). Districts do not grow across a river, so a boundary forms along each; it is laid on the river and widened to hold it, with a green bank and a path along each side, and the streets and rail lines that cross it are bridges. Districts stop at the sea, with a waterfront park and a promenade along the shore.

- **Districts.** District cores come first, spread like a loose Poisson field with varied spacing. Districts grow outward from each core over 400 m land parcels, and where districts meet the boundary becomes a primary greenway. Boundaries carry detail at several scales: broad sweeps and meanders from layered warps, then straight runs of 110 to 340 m with rounded bends, and small swells and pinches in width. Greenways carry no streams.
- **Boulevards.** Tram boulevards link the cores of neighbouring districts. Each link crosses the shared greenway near its middle at right angles; inside a district the links join through the station or end at T junctions (roundabouts are kept for roads outside the city). Boulevards and main streets that meet on a greenway carry on under it, drawn as tunnels.
- **District parks** of 40 to 70 ha sit where three districts meet, spread out evenly. Each flows out along its greenways and keeps a straight edge where it meets a boulevard.
- **Neighbourhoods.** Each is planned for about 15 000 people, enough to support its own shops, schools and services. Density varies, so areas do too: the mid-rise core around the station is densest, boulevards a little denser, and each neighbourhood is built in one form: the less dense (under 18 500 people per km²) are rowhouses throughout, with mixed-use blocks along their main streets, the rest perimeter blocks. Houses of three storeys or more are split into flats, as plexes are. About one district in three has a campus neighbourhood (university or hospital) that houses fewer people for its size. A district gets as many neighbourhoods as its people fill. Every neighbourhood gets a main street through its centre first, and is then regrown outward from that street, so the main street nearest a point decides which neighbourhood it is in and boundaries fall between main streets. Hovering shows a neighbourhood's housing, population and density. Close in, once blocks are in, every block belongs to one neighbourhood, so boundaries run down the middle of streets (smoothed so they do not step round single blocks), and each neighbourhood is planned for the people its own blocks hold.
- **Main streets** (31 m, tram) are old roads between places, forming an organic grid. Every neighbourhood gets one route through its centre (at least one main street, and rarely more than three), laid from the core outwards: its inner half heads for the core and ends in a T on the first street that carries on past it, and its outer half runs on to the next neighbourhood further out, or to a gateway on the greenway (one per shared greenway, two on long ones), where it carries on into the next district. A neighbourhood still without one gets a cross street through its centre; in a wedge of land between two greenways it may end at the gate of the district park they meet at. A smooth direction field follows the boulevards and greenway edges, leans towards each core and drifts slowly elsewhere, so streets bend gently and meet mostly at right angles. No main street dead-ends, curls round or runs alongside another, and junctions keep clear of each other. With about 15 000 people to a neighbourhood that comes to roughly 1.8 to 2 km of main street and boulevard per km² (a main street every kilometre or so), with nearly all land within 600 m of one.
- **Local streets and blocks** (stage 4, from 0.25 px/m in). Streets come first, as centrelines, and each takes its own width out of the land either side, so a block is what is left between the building lines and a street is exactly the space between buildings. Main streets, boulevards and the streets along every greenway and park cut a district into superblocks; each is split again and again by a local street running across its long side, curving to meet the streets at its ends square on and carrying on any street already meeting it from outside, until the pieces are the size their housing calls for: perimeter blocks of about 100 m, rowhouse blocks about 50 m deep and 150 m or more long, larger podium blocks round the station, campus blocks. Every local street ends on another street; junctions are crossings and T junctions at about right angles, with no short jogs. Each neighbourhood's square (with a market) fronts its main street by its centre. A square may stand on its own with a pedestrian street off it, or take in the local street on one side, or on two adjacent sides, so it runs right up to the buildings of the blocks across; a street it takes in goes on beyond it as a pedestrian street. Each neighbourhood's park and secondary school sit side by side, set back from the main streets. Local streets show their pavements close in.
- **Plots** (from 1 px/m in). A perimeter block is several buildings round a courtyard, with gaps of 4.5 to 6.5 m between them, at least one on every street, open through to the courtyard. In the courtyard a path runs round along the backs of the buildings, and paths cross the green from the gaps to a small square in the middle. Plots are cut square to the street (and in a block with no courtyard, square to its long sides), narrower for mixed use on a main street, with corner plots wrapping round their corners. A rowhouse block splits down its spine into two terraces of houses 5 to 8 m wide, in Toronto's proportions: a 3 to 5 m front garden, a house 14 to 17 m deep, a short backyard opening onto a 5 m lane down the spine, running street to street. Corner houses face the long street. Rowhouse blocks are smaller than perimeter blocks (about 200 by 86 m to the street centrelines), so rowhouse areas have more streets; a block too far from rectangular for terraces is split again, and a small awkward leftover is a green. Podium blocks in the core are cut into a few big plots.
- **Buildings** (stage 5, from 1 px/m in). Each building stands on a run of plots (between two gaps round a courtyard, or a whole terrace) and has one height, style and roof, ridges running along the street: a footprint, a height from its storeys, an architectural style, and a roof. On main streets buildings stand on the building line; on quieter streets a few step back a metre or two and their backs vary in depth; rowhouses have front gardens; the core is a street wall of podiums with a tower at about one corner in three, set back on its podium, now and then behind a forecourt. Styles are traditional European for the most part (Georgian, Victorian, neoclassical, Renaissance and Baroque revival, Beaux-Arts, Art Nouveau, Art Deco): each district has a family of them, each neighbourhood leans to one, a block is mostly built in one. Schools, hospitals, recreation centres and towers are modern; churches have spires, mosques domes and minarets, and each district has a cathedral, cross-shaped in its close beside a square near the core. Under Buildings in the panel, heights show as shadows (2D) or as raised buildings seen from the south (3D), with roofs and walls in their materials or plain. Click a building for a card with what it is, its height, style, homes and shops.
- **Streets to scale** (from 1.1 px/m in). Every street is drawn as its cross-section from the street types: pavements, tree strips with street trees, cycle tracks, kerbs, traffic lanes, tram tracks with their rails, planted medians; junctions are paved across. Tram stops stand on boulevards and main streets about every 300 m, at junctions with other main streets first, then beside squares, schools, hospitals, the cathedral and the station; on a main street the tracks part round an island platform, on a boulevard there is a platform each side. Farther out, stops show as small markers.
- **Greenways and parks** (stage 5). A primary greenway is left natural but for a stream and a trail. The stream runs down the middle, drifting a little off it and back as a small river does, and takes heavy rain off the streets; where greenways meet their streams join, and their trails meet there too; the trail, wide enough for people walking and cycling both ways, follows it loosely on one side; Local streets that meet a greenway send a path down to the trail every 140 m or so, and each pavement of a street dipping under a greenway has a path down to it. Parkettes have a path and, in the bigger ones, a playground. A neighbourhood park is a mesh of paths, from entrances at its corners and along its sides to junctions through the park, with the neighbourhood's community centre built into its edge on a street. A school's grounds take its track and field (or a field) first, and the building stands in the room left, near the street. District parks add playing fields, tennis and basketball courts, a dog park, food kiosks, a pond, sometimes an outdoor pool or a woodland cemetery and trails. Some neighbourhood boundaries (not most: usually a boundary is just a street) are secondary greenways: linear parks 28 to 40 m wide running from one main street to the next, or to a primary greenway, along the boundary, with a trail down the middle and trees along both sides; streets run along their sides and local streets meet them rather than crossing, with a path across where they do. More than half the districts have a stadium (also a concert venue) on a block of its own near the core: an oval, a rounded rectangle or a horseshoe of stands round a pitch.
- **Rail and metro** (stage 5). Regional rail runs on viaducts above the boulevards, each district joined to its neighbour nearer the city centre (the core nearest the middle of the site), so lines run out from the centre through every district; each district has its station at the core and, on a long run towards the centre, one more stop. The metro runs in tunnels between neighbouring districts the rail does not join. At each core its links pair up with the one most nearly opposite, so lines run straight on through as smooth curves, crossing about square where two share a station (an interchange); a link with no partner ends at the core. There is a station at each core and one more between each core and the next, at a neighbourhood centre near the line where there is one, so stations are about a kilometre apart. Both show under Rail stations in the panel.
- **Names** (stage 5). Districts, neighbourhoods and streets get English names, put together from word lists the way English names are made: old place names (a root and an ending, as Ashford or Kingsley; or Market Heath, Old Thornwick), family names, trees and landmarks. Boulevards are Avenues or Boulevards; main streets are Streets, and keep their name across a greenway; local streets are mostly Streets, with the odd Way or Road, Lanes, Mews and Places when short, Crescents when curved; pedestrian streets are Walks or Rows. No two streets in a district share a name. Labels follow their streets and keep the right way up; district names show far out, neighbourhood names in between, street names close in.
- **What is where**, following the urban design guide. Plots on main streets, boulevards, pedestrian streets and squares are mixed use: a shop floor, offices or studios above, homes over that; off them, quieter shop floors on corners and some other plots; in rowhouse areas only corner shops, and a parkette every 250 m or so. Every neighbourhood has its square and market, park, secondary school, two elementary schools, community centre, inn, places of worship (in about Toronto's mix), post office, food bank, health, arts, entertainment, food, shops and services along its main street; about one in three is a subdistrict centre (library, fire station, recreation centre, performing arts, cinema); the core has the district's own (hotels, office towers, police, library, museum, hospitals nearby, now and then a department store or courthouse). Every home is within 400 m of a grocery, convenience store, café, bank, gym, daycare, clinic and pharmacy. Storeys are set so each neighbourhood houses about the people it was planned for. Coloured dots (from 1.6 px/m in) show shop floors; hovering shows what is on a plot.

Far-zoom tiles render in Web Workers.

## Run

ES modules need a server, so `file://` won't work:

```
python3 -m http.server 8123
```

Then open http://localhost:8123. Views are shareable: `?seed=1&pop=1500000&x=1400&y=1400&zoom=0.05`.

## Publish

A GitHub Pages workflow (`.github/workflows/pages.yml`) runs the tests and publishes the site on every push to `main`. Once, in the repository settings under Pages, set Source to GitHub Actions. The site then lives at `https://<user>.github.io/post-car-city/`.

## Test

```
node --test
```

## Conventions

- Units are metres. World x grows east, y grows south (north is up).
- No `Math.random()` in generation. Use `hash(seed, layer, ...ints)` or `rngFor(...)` from `src/core`.
- Generation lives in `src/core` and `src/world`; drawing in `src/render`; interface in `src/ui`.
