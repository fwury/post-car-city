# Post-Car City: plan

The work queued next, in order, with every decision taken so far. Each
phase builds on the ones before it. Nothing here is started yet.

## Phase 1. Quick fixes (done)

1. **Minimum population 750 000** in the population option, so regional
   rail, metro and a city centre all make sense.
2. **Regional rail fully underground.** A 3% grade means going from a
   viaduct to an underground station takes about 1 km of ramp, which cuts
   the urban fabric. At 20 000/km², bury the whole line inside the
   built-up area.
   - Stations only at district centres: no infill stops in parks or
     empty land.
   - Not every district needs a rail station; keep the current method of
     choosing them. But **every district centre must be served by
     regional rail or metro**.
   - District stations are RER/metro style, underground, with entrances
     only. **No station building or plaza except the city-centre central
     station.**
   - A district's rail station sits right under its main intersection
     and **is shared with its metro station**.
   - Rail lines no longer divide neighbourhoods; streets run straight
     over them. Check this is fully the case.

## Phase 2. Street structure

3. **Neighbourhood boundaries that make sense.** Main streets are the main
   way of dividing land. Some neighbourhoods must be bounded by main
   streets on every side (needed for the old town).
4. **Main street generator v3.**
   - It's fine for main streets to be denser than one per neighbourhood,
     **as long as they are not bunched up**. Density is driven by tram
     stop coverage (Phase 4), not by population per km.
   - Every neighbourhood has a main street running through its middle,
     with its amenities on it.
   - Roughly parallel main streets stay **at least 500 m** apart. Never
     parallel close together; never twisting round one another; never
     overlapping.
   - Main streets meet only at proper junctions, at **60° or more**, and
     ideally most at right angles.
   - No main street (or tram route) runs along the very edge of a
     district.
   - The network must stay **organic and natural**, as a street grid
     grows. A simple radial network out from the district centre would
     work but is unnatural; avoid it.
   - A district centre may carry more main street than elsewhere, still
     without overlaps or close parallels.
   - To think through: other ways to bring everyone near a stop besides
     adding main street (stops placed for coverage, routes using local
     tram streets through gaps, and so on).

## Phase 3. City-centre district

5. **Neighbourhood roles**, decided before blocks are built: the business
   district, the old town, the main university campus (Phase 5), and the
   rest. At 750k and up (so in every city), the other city-centre
   neighbourhoods are all perimeter blocks, never rowhouses.

6. **Business district** (the city-centre district's own centre, round
   the central station).
   - Its name is generated from a list, with common names weighted more
     heavily: e.g. Financial District, Business District, New Town,
     Downtown, The City, Exchange. "The Loop" is Chicago-specific and
     should be rare.
   - It is all podium blocks with towers. Unlike other district centres,
     towers hold many offices (corporate headquarters) as well as homes.
     Mixed use throughout.
   - It has several plazas:
     - the station plaza in front of the central station (which will
       later also take high-speed rail);
     - the district plaza.
   - It still gets a normal amount of park.
   - **All district centres, this one included, get more parkettes** to
     make up for having few or no perimeter blocks with their courtyards.
   - Flagship hotels and the city's main convention centre are here.
   - **Observation tower**: a dedicated building on its own block, with a
     plaza all round it, in the style of Tokyo Skytree, CN Tower, Canton
     Tower or Shanghai Tower.

7. **Old town**: one neighbourhood next to the business district, the one
   with the most metro service.
   - **Entirely pedestrianised**, all older buildings.
   - Bounded by main streets on every side.
   - **Must have a metro station at its centre.**
   - Messy perimeter blocks, smaller than usual. Each is made of separate
     buildings round its edge, in one architectural style but varying
     more, like a European old town; similar to the very first draft of
     the blocks.
   - Holds:
     - the main library;
     - the tourist centre;
     - more boutique hotels (in the perimeter blocks, no big dedicated
       buildings);
     - museums;
     - the district plaza, with **city hall facing it**;
     - a few smaller plazas and markets.
   - **Pedestrian main street**, the same width as a normal main street.
     From the building face inward on each side:
     - a wide pedestrian clearway (a tourist destination);
     - a 1.2 m furnishing zone (benches, signs);
     - a 2.1 m cycle lane;
     - a wider furnishing zone, wide enough to hold a level-boarding
       tram platform at stops;
     - then the tram tracks in the centre.

     There are no vehicle lanes and no median. **Trams stop here**, at
     platforms level with the tram for accessibility. No pedestrian
     crossings are needed.

8. **Facilities in the city-centre district.**
   - Placed at a neighbourhood centre **near a metro station**:
     - **Main courthouse**, next to a courthouse plaza.
     - **Research hospital(s)**, on top of the district's usual
       hospitals. In larger cities, by chance per seed (about 30% at 2M
       rising to 80% at 8M), a cluster of specialised hospitals across a
       neighbourhood, like Toronto's Discovery District, ideally next to
       the campus. In some seeds the neighbourhood is named "… Medical
       District", "… Research District", "… Discovery District", "…
       Innovation District" (more to think of).
     - **Main performing arts centre** (very large) with a plaza; an
       **opera house** with a plaza where the city is big enough. In some
       larger cities (same chance curve), a performing arts cluster of
       theatres along a neighbourhood's main streets instead, like
       Broadway, the West End or Avenida Corrientes. In some seeds named
       "… Theatre District", "… Entertainment District", "… Cultural
       District", "… Arts District", or iconic enough to need no suffix.
   - Scattered along main streets: **embassies and consulates**.

## Phase 4. Trams v2

9. **Centre-to-centre routes.** Two adjacent districts with no metro or
   regional rail link **must** have a direct tram route between their
   centres. Ideally adjacent districts that do have such a link also get
   one, since the modes serve different purposes.
10. **Coverage: 95%+ of residents within 400 m (straight line) of a tram
    stop**, ideally everyone; the city is planned from scratch. This
    stands in for the usual 500 m walking guideline, which is costly to
    test. Reached mostly through the denser main streets of Phase 2, then
    local tram streets where gaps remain.
11. **Most neighbourhoods are joined to their district centre by a direct
    tram route.** This is already planned.
12. **Route numbers** shown on routes once zoomed in far enough (one or
    several per street).
13. **Routes drawn side by side**, like Apple Maps: each route its own
    line where they share a street.

Notes from the first version: the two-district 300k city joined only 67%
of its neighbourhoods. It falls under the new 750k minimum anyway.

## Phase 5. Higher education

14. **Main university campus** in the city centre, one in every city,
    taking a whole neighbourhood.
    - The city's premier research institution, with a specific name:
      "University of (City)", "(Name) University", "(Name) College" and
      the like.
    - **Pedestrianised**, by the same rules as the old town, with a
      **metro station**.
    - A mix of older buildings (like Trinity/UC at UofT, UCL, ICL,
      Oxford, Cambridge) and modern ones. All interiors modern and
      spacious.
    - **A central lawn** surrounded by large traditional buildings (like
      UofT's Front Campus): the auditorium, bookstore, library, museums.
    - **Buildings it has:**
      - a central library (old style, large);
      - a convocation hall or auditorium (old style, large);
      - museums (old style, medium to large);
      - an athletic centre (modern);
      - a student union or centre (old style, smaller);
      - a bookstore (old style);
      - a health clinic;
      - a dedicated stadium;
      - places of worship of various faiths, each in a fitting style.
    - **Student residences**, the number by university size. A mix of:
      - traditional quadrangle perimeter blocks;
      - modern podium-and-tower blocks with mixed-use ground floors. These
        hold only the everyday amenities students need (bank, restaurants,
        cafés), with little retail.
    - **Department buildings**: mostly modern, humanities more often
      traditional.
      - **Labelled** with their department, e.g. "Life Sciences
        Building", "Department of Life Sciences", "(Name) Life Sciences
        Building", "(Name) Building", "… Institute", "… Lab". Not too
        random.
      - **Names after people**: the share varies by campus on an inverse
        bell curve. Most campuses name almost none after people, or
        almost all.
      - **Enough to host basically every major programme.** UofT
        equivalents:
        - Life Sciences (Ramsay Wright), modern
        - Chemistry (Miller Chemical Labs), modern
        - Physical Sciences (McLennan), modern
        - Computer Science (Bahen), modern
        - Engineering (Myhal), modern
        - Business (Rotman), modern
        - Medicine (Medical Sciences Building), modern
        - Education (OISE), modern
        - Law
        - Social Sciences (AP, SK)
        - Humanities (Jackman), older; may split into English, History,
          Languages and so on
        - Social Work and Public Policy (FIFSW, Munk)
        - General lecture halls (Sidney Smith)
        - Science library (Gerstein, UCL Science Library)
      - **Larger campuses may add**: architecture, music, drama, visual
        arts, a cultural centre (Hart House), theatres; dentistry,
        optometry, pharmacy; and dedicated research buildings (Donnelly
        Centre).
      - **Vary by campus.** Smaller ones combine buildings: humanities
        with social sciences, life sciences with medicine, no separate
        science library. Larger ones split them: separate engineering
        buildings (UofT's MC, MB, SF, GB, WB, RS), Anthropology, Anatomy,
        Language and Culture, Neuroscience (UCL).
    - Every campus differs by seed.
15. **Other universities**: about **one full university per million
    residents** in all, with some variation by seed.
    - Smaller campuses are **still pedestrianised**, but smaller than a
      neighbourhood. Give each its own smaller-than-usual neighbourhood,
      so it doesn't undercut the minimum requirements of the
      neighbourhood it would otherwise eat into.
    - Every university has a specific name.
16. **Non-university higher education**: about **twice as many as
    universities** in all.
    - Mostly **colleges, polytechnics and trade schools**.
    - Specialised schools appear only in larger cities, **at most one of
      each kind per city**: art and design (OCAD), music conservatory
      (Juilliard) or combined music and theatre, film and acting,
      culinary, seminary, single-subject schools such as a standalone law
      school.
    - Each takes **one plot, or 2–3 adjacent plots**, on a main street.

## Phase 6. City-wide facilities and the stadium fix

17. Where the city is large enough to justify them, within the built-up
    area:

| Facility | How many / where |
|---|---|
| Aquarium | preferably in the city-centre district |
| Fairgrounds / amusement areas | in district parks, 1 per 500k; the area only, no rides or paths drawn |
| Athletic training centres | 1 per million |
| Stadiums | 1 per million (fix stadiums citywide) |
| Mental health hospital | preferably city centre; 60 beds per million, so from about 3M |
| Adult secondary schools | 1 per 50 000, spread evenly across districts |
| Corrective facilities | progressive/Scandinavian style, lower security, 1 per million |
| Small zoos | in district parks, about 1 per million |

## Phase 7. Periphery

18. **Greenbelt**: a naturalised green buffer **1–2 km wide** between the
    built-up edge and the expressway, scaling with city size. Transit
    still crosses it to reach the industrial areas.
    - Sparse, leisurely hiking trails (like Rouge Park).
    - Holds, where the city justifies it: a large zoo, a large amusement
      park (Canada's Wonderland), a botanical garden or nature
      conservatory, a racetrack.
    - These stand **well back from roads** (noise, traffic, danger).
    - They are **served by metro**, since lines run out to the industrial
      areas and airport anyway, and later by peripheral buses.
    - Room is left for later expansion that isn't directly next to the
      city. Satellite towns come later.
19. **Expressway**: a ring, or a simple peripheral road where water or
    mountains make a ring pointless.
    - Four lanes each way, 3.5 m each, the inside lane HOV.
    - A wide grass central median with a concrete barrier.
20. **Roads**: these never enter the built-up area.
    - Each becomes a boulevard at the built-up edge. Only some boulevards
      continue out as roads.
    - They carry motor traffic from the expressway and industrial areas
      onto the boulevards.
    - Some end at a trumpet junction on the expressway. Others cross it
      at a diverging diamond and continue to the industrial areas.
    - Each road has a multi-use trail, fully grade-separated and a safe
      distance from it.
    - Cross-section: the existing `road` type.
21. **Heavy industrial areas** beyond the expressway.
    - About **one per 1–1.5 million residents**, each about 2–5 km².
      Each is named.
    - Served by metro and/or regional rail (they are employment hubs).
    - A large regular grid of industrial streets, built for trucks.
    - May hold:
      - heavy and light manufacturing;
      - processing plants;
      - distribution centres;
      - cold storage;
      - truck terminals and cross-docks;
      - storage units;
      - power stations;
      - utilities (water treatment, waste transfer, recycling);
      - rail depots (freight, regional/intercity, metro, tram);
      - bus barns;
      - vehicle dealerships;
      - data centres.
    - Plan their layout before building them.
    - Open question below: whether some get a neighbourhood of their own.
22. **Park-and-ride**: where a rail line that continues beyond the city
    (as intercity rail) meets the expressway, a large parking facility at
    a regional rail station, clearly named as a park-and-ride.

## Phase 8. Airports

23. **Main airport** for cities of 1M+, outside the expressway.
    - Size and complexity scale with population: one terminal for smaller
      cities, several for larger ones.
    - **Served by regional rail (an existing line extended outward, not a
      branch) and by a metro line.**
    - Reachable by car, since its catchment includes rural areas, but car
      access is not the primary mode.
    - Its own work session.
24. **General aviation airfield** beyond the expressway, from **3M**.
25. Later: ferry terminals, cruise terminals and seaports for large
    coastal cities.

## Phase 9. Water

26. **Natural rivers.**
    - Follow real river dynamics (read up on them): width that varies,
      meanders, braids, tributaries and streams joining.
    - Islands, by chance and by river size (Île de la Cité, Île
      Saint-Louis, Montreal where two rivers meet), with bridges and
      built-up land on them.
    - Canal systems in some seeds.
27. **Waterfronts.**
    - **Rivers**: in the city centre and inner districts (how far out
      depends on city size), pedestrianised lower banks (Paris), plazas
      at the water's edge (Porto) and boardwalks (Huangpu). Outer
      districts get continuous trails (Vancouver seawall).
    - **Sea**: quays (Sydney) and beaches in the centre; beaches and
      trails further out. Plus piers, bluffs (Scarborough) and other
      interesting terrain.
    - **A district touching a river most likely has its centre right on
      the river**, possibly with its district plaza on the water.
28. **Growth along water**: river and coastal cities stretch along the
    river or coast more than away from it. Feasible: weight the order in
    which districts join the city (and the city shape noise) by
    closeness to the water, so the built-up area elongates along it.
    Do it together with Phase 9.

## Phase 10. Leftovers

29. **Bike share stations** (pending from before). Small icons only, no
    docks drawn, at:
    - every rail and metro station and tram stop (on the main street
      sidewalk or a side street);
    - every neighbourhood park (in the park by a path, near a corner);
    - each district park entrance;
    - wherever a primary greenway meets a main street or boulevard
      (beside the greenway).
30. **Bus routes** for the peripheral, non-built-up areas, the greenbelt
    attractions included.

## Open question

**A neighbourhood next to large industrial areas?** The idea: a single
neighbourhood out near the industrial areas beyond the expressway, served
by metro, only for the largest areas (30 000+ jobs).

- **Against it:**
  - it is isolated across the greenbelt and expressway, with fewer
    amenities and districts around it;
  - it risks becoming a working-class enclave;
  - it sits next to heavy industry's noise and pollution.
- **Instead:** excellent transit from the built-up edge, with metro
  stations inside the industrial area and frequent service. Then everyone
  in the city can reach those jobs, and workers can live anywhere.

**Recommendation:** transit only for now. Reconsider a mixed neighbourhood
(housing for all incomes plus light industry and offices, not just worker
housing) later, alongside satellite towns.

## Ideas for later (inspiration, not to be packed into every seed)

Pick a few per seed, where they fit; they need not all be coherent.

- **Street life**
  - Alley districts of tiny bars and restaurants (Omoide Yokocho).
  - Covered shopping arcades (Galerie Vivienne).
  - Night markets; street food streets and districts.
  - Live music and bar streets (Jiuyanqiao, El Poblado, Sukhumvit), or
    whole entertainment districts (Dotonbori).
  - Small designated districts along a stretch of main street in larger
    cities: electronics (Akihabara), fashion (Harajuku), art studios
    (798), exhibition grounds (the Ex), horticulture (Kew).
- **Hills and mountains**
  - Stair streets and winding hillside shopping paths (Valparaíso,
    Chongqing).
  - Funiculars (Lyon); outdoor escalators and hillside lifts (Hong Kong,
    Lisbon).
- **Buildings**
  - District-centre towers with podium retail and offices and
    office/residential towers.
  - Sky gardens, rooftop gardens and viewing decks (Marina One, Shibuya
    Sky), terraces on podium roofs.
  - Public courtyards in cultural buildings (British Museum Great Court);
    a mix of large and small museums.
- **Public space and parks**
  - Large squares with gardens or pools (Trocadéro).
  - Large destination parks with ponds and gardens (Inokashira, Hyde
    Park, Central Park, Luxembourg, Güell), beyond the uniform district
    parks.
  - Community greenhouses and urban farms in parks.
  - Public washrooms in every neighbourhood park.
  - Elevated greenways built from scratch (High Line, Singapore Rail
    Corridor).
- **Water**
  - Riverfront amphitheatres (Lyon); public piers (Navy Pier).
  - Canals (Amsterdam, Venice, Birmingham); urban ferries (Istanbul,
    London).
- **Culture and terrain**
  - Cultural enclaves, with naming systems in other languages as a
    setting. Needs every kind of name generated per language.
  - Terrain: mountains, archipelagos, islands, peninsulas, beaches,
    hills.
